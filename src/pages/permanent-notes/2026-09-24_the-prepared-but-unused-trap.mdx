A few weeks ago I audited my own output. Documents written. Demos rehearsed. Questions drafted for meetings, carefully staged, thoughtfully worded. Then a harder question: which of these did anyone ever see? The answer was uncomfortable enough to become this post. Everything I prepare tends to stall one step before it does any good — and it never feels like failure from the inside.

## The trap has a name

Chinese has a four-character idiom, 备而不用 — "stockpiled, never deployed." It's usually praise: be prepared, and you'll never be caught out. But for someone who builds systems for a living, I've been living its failure mode. Call it the prepared-but-unused trap: the state where you keep producing artifacts — documents, demos, write-ups — and none of them complete their journey. Not to other people, and not even back into your own head.

The trap is sticky because every individual step feels virtuous. Preparation is credit without risk: you feel the progress of a finished document without paying the exposure cost of showing it. And showing carries a real-looking tax. My quiet version of it was an equation: presenting my work = packaging = advertising = slightly fake. The equation breaks the moment you check it against the evidence: the work was real, and you did it. Presenting real work isn't packaging. It's the last step of doing it.

## The gears: why smart people stockpile

The trap doesn't come from laziness. Three mechanisms hold it together.

First, preparation is safe in a way that sharing isn't. A finished document in a private folder can still be imagined as excellent. The moment it ships, it can be measured — and measurement can hurt. So the system drifts toward the step that can't fail.

Second, the packaging tax compounds the first. If somewhere you absorbed the belief that visible work is less authentic, then every act of sharing carries a hidden moral cost. You don't procrastinate from laziness; you procrastinate from integrity — wrongly priced.

Third, and this is the one engineers will recognize: unused content doesn't loop back. A demo you never gave didn't just fail to reach an audience; it never re-entered your own head, so it never compounded into memory, judgment, or confidence. Content that isn't used doesn't sit still. It depreciates. The chapter of my book about note-taking names the same mechanism: if your thinking only changes your notes, it's academic. If it changes decisions, it's wisdom. The prepared-but-unused trap is that failure mode wearing the costume of diligence.

## The last-mile rule

The fix is not "promote yourself more." Volume isn't the problem; the missing last step is. So the rule I now run on every artifact:

**Every prepared thing ends in a used moment — posted, spoken, or fed back into my own head. If it can't name its used moment, it isn't done.**

Three forms qualify. Shipped outward: the summary that goes out the same day, because sharing that costs zero courage is the only kind that happens reliably. Spoken: the one sentence in the room that surfaces the work — which requires the material to have gone through your mind first. Fed back: the notes you actually re-read before tomorrow's decisions, so yesterday's thinking prepares tomorrow's work instead of rotting in a folder.

Notice what the rule does to the packaging fear. It doesn't ask you to perform or inflate. It asks the work to take its final step. You did the work, so showing it is not an ad. It's a delivery receipt.

## Generation needs a destination

There is a 2026 twist that makes this trap industrial-scale. AI has removed almost all the cost of generating content. Documents, summaries, demos, briefs — a good setup can produce them faster than anyone can read them. Which means the trap no longer needs your procrastination to fill up; it fills itself.

So I've added one more discipline, and it might be the most important line in this post: generation needs a declared destination. Before anything gets produced, it should know which of two consumers it serves. Your head — in which case it must be shaped to be absorbed: short, spoken, spaced out, sized for a commute. Or the room — in which case it must be shaped to be understood together: paste-ready, questions included, nothing that needs a private briefing to decode. Content generated for neither destination isn't preparation. It's landfill with good formatting. The discipline applies to the machine's output too: if a sentence can't name where it lands, don't ship it.

## The compounding you've been missing

Here is what finally convinced me to change: confidence doesn't accrue on preparation. It only accrues on shipped moments. You can stockpile for a year and feel no surer of yourself; give one demo, post one document, and something settles. The prepared-but-unused trap is expensive precisely because it feels free — every unshipped artifact paid no interest, and the account it starves is your own self-trust.

So, the question I'll leave with you: what is sitting finished-but-unused in your drive right now — and what is its used moment, this week?
